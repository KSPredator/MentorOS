### Chapter 1: Introduction to Foundation Models & AI Engineering

1. **What is the primary shift from traditional ML Engineering to AI Engineering?** The focus moves from training bespoke models from scratch to building applications on top of pre-trained foundation models.
2. **What defines a "Foundation Model"?** A large-scale model trained on vast amounts of unlabelled data that can be adapted for a wide range of downstream tasks.
3. **What are the three layers of the AI Engineering Stack?** The Model layer (foundation models), the Orchestration layer (prompting, tools, agents), and the Application layer (business logic and UI).
4. **How do Large Language Models (LLMs) differ from earlier models?** They scale massively in parameters and data, which unlocks emergent capabilities beyond simple pattern recognition.
5. **What is the "Crawl, Walk, Run" framework for AI automation?** Crawl (human involvement is mandatory), Walk (AI interacts directly with internal employees), Run (increased automation and direct AI interactions with external users).
6. **What characterizes "Critical AI"?** Use cases where high accuracy and reliability are mandatory, such as medical or financial tasks.
7. **What characterizes "Complementary AI"?** Use cases where users are more accepting of occasional mistakes because the AI acts as an assistant or brainstorming partner.
8. **What is the difference between dynamic and static AI features?** Static features use one model serving a group of users and update periodically; dynamic features require personalization or per-user models.
9. **What is the lowest-effort technique to adapt a foundation model?** Prompt Engineering.
10. **What is the most resource-intensive technique to adapt a model?** Fine-tuning, as it requires significant task-specific data and high compute effort.
11. **What are the three levels of risk when adopting AI?** Existential threat (competitors obsolete you), Missed opportunity (loss of productivity), and Strategic hedging (fear of being left behind).
12. **Why is AI engineering different from full-stack engineering?** AI engineering requires managing probabilistic systems, non-deterministic outputs, and continuous evaluation, whereas traditional software relies on deterministic logic.

### Chapter 2: Understanding Foundation Models

13. **What is a "masked language model"?** A model (like BERT) trained to predict a missing token within a sequence by looking at context both before and after the gap.
14. **What is an "autoregressive language model"?** A model trained to predict the next token given only the preceding tokens (e.g., GPT series).
15. **What phase of training consumes the most compute resources?** Pre-training, which often accounts for roughly 98% of total compute resources.
16. **Who typically performs fine-tuning?** Application developers tailoring a pre-trained model to specific application tasks.
17. **What is the core architecture underlying modern LLMs?** The Transformer architecture.
18. **What are the two main components of a Transformer block?** The Attention module and the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) module.
19. **What is the primary advantage of the Attention mechanism?** It allows the model to weigh the relevance of all other words in the input sequence when processing a specific token.
20. **How does input processing differ from output generation in Transformers?** Inputs can be processed in parallel, but outputs must be decoded sequentially.
21. **What is an SSM?** State Space Models, an alternative architecture aiming to handle longer context efficiently without the bottlenecks of standard attention.
22. **What is Post-Training?** Adjustments made after pre-training by model developers, such as instruction tuning or preference alignment.
23. **Why is data quality crucial during pre-training?** The model absorbs the facts, linguistic structures, and biases present in the foundational training data.
24. **What is the fundamental cause of LLM inconsistency?** They are probabilistic systems; the same input can yield slightly or drastically different outputs based on sampling dynamics.
25. **What is sampling in model generation?** The process of selecting the next token from the probability distribution generated by the model.
26. **How can developers mitigate output inconsistency?** By fixing sampling parameters, using memory structures, or caching input/output pairs.
27. **What are structured outputs?** Forcing the model to return data in a strict schema (like JSON) so it can be reliably parsed by deterministic code.
28. **What is "Test Time Compute"?** Strategies that give the model more time to process or generate intermediate steps before producing a final answer, improving reasoning.
29. **What is a "token" in the context of LLMs?** A sub-word unit of text; on average, a token is roughly 3/4 of a standard English word.
30. **What is the role of preference finetuning?** To align the model’s behavior with human preferences (e.g., being helpful and harmless) rather than just completing text.

### Chapter 3: Evaluation Methodology

31. **Why is evaluating foundation models inherently difficult?** Outputs are open-ended, and there is often no single "correct" answer, unlike traditional classification tasks.
32. **What is "Exact Evaluation"?** Checking for functional correctness, similar to unit tests—such as verifying if generated code compiles.
33. **What is Cross Entropy?** A mathematical metric measuring the difference between the model's predicted probability distribution and the actual dataset.
34. **What is Perplexity?** A measurement of how well a probability model predicts a sample; lower perplexity means the model is less "surprised" by the text.
35. **When are similarity measurements used?** For tasks where exact evaluation fails, output is compared against reference data using algorithmic similarity.
36. **What is "AI as a Judge"?** A technique where another, usually stronger, AI model evaluates the outputs based on a specific grading rubric.
37. **What is a major limitation of "AI as a Judge"?** It can be inconsistent, increases latency/costs, and lacks standardized scoring criteria (criteria ambiguity).
38. **Why is human evaluation considered the gold standard?** Because humans can intuitively grasp context, usefulness, and nuance better than automatic metrics.
39. **What does "groundedness" measure?** Whether the model's output is supported strictly by the provided context.
40. **What is faithfulness in evaluation?** Ensuring the model does not contradict the provided source material.
41. **Why evaluate "harmfulness"?** To prevent the model from generating toxic, biased, explicit, or malicious content.
42. **What is the downside of continuous human evaluation?** It is slow, expensive, and difficult to scale across rapid deployment cycles.
43. **What is "spot-checking"?** Randomly sampling a subset of model outputs for evaluation to monitor quality without evaluating every single generation.
44. **What is criteria ambiguity?** The challenge where evaluators interpret subjective guidelines (like "helpfulness") differently.
45. **What do automatic metrics lack?** They are fast but often fail to reflect real-world usefulness or semantic accuracy.

### Chapter 4: Evaluate AI Systems

46. **What does TTFT stand for?** Time to First Token, a critical latency metric for user experience.
47. **What does TPOT stand for?** Time per Output Token, measuring generation speed.
48. **Why is TTFT critical for conversational bots?** A fast TTFT signals to the user that the system is responsive, reducing perceived waiting time.
49. **How is cost typically measured in LLM APIs?** Cost per inference request, usually calculated per thousand or million tokens.
50. **What is pass@1 in code generation?** An evaluation metric that measures the probability that the very first code snippet generated passes all required tests.
51. **What is the Elo score in model evaluation?** An overall model quality metric adapted from chess, used to rank models based on head-to-head preference.
52. **What factors influence the "Build Versus Buy" model decision?** Data privacy, API cost versus engineering cost, copyright lineage, and model performance.
53. **What is a major risk of relying on a commercial provider's API?** Vendor risk—the provider may go out of business, deprecate models, or drastically change pricing.
54. **What is Step 1 of designing an evaluation pipeline?** Evaluate all components in the system individually (turn-based versus task-based).
55. **What is Step 2 of designing an evaluation pipeline?** Create an evaluation guideline that strictly defines criteria for what "good" looks like.
56. **What is Step 3 of designing an evaluation pipeline?** Define and select the evaluation methods and secure the evaluation dataset.
57. **Why must evaluation pipelines track failures?** To identify edge cases and implement fallback strategies when the AI system fails.
58. **How do public benchmarks assist model selection?** They provide a baseline comparison of how models handle domain-specific capabilities and instruction-following.
59. **Why monitor latency and cost per task in production?** Because these metrics heavily dictate the economic viability and user retention of the AI application.
60. **What does consistency evaluation check?** Whether the system delivers reliable outputs over time and across slightly different phrasing of the same task.

### Chapter 5: Prompt Engineering

61. **What is Prompt Engineering?** Crafting explicit instructions and examples to guide a model's output without altering its underlying weights.
62. **What is Zero-Shot prompting?** Asking the model to perform a task using in-context learning without providing any examples.
63. **What is Few-Shot prompting?** Providing a few examples of inputs and desired outputs within the prompt to guide the model's formatting and logic.
64. **What is the difference between a System Prompt and a User Prompt?** The system prompt sets the overarching behavior and rules, while the user prompt contains the specific immediate query.
65. **Why should you write clear and explicit instructions?** Because models are probabilistic; ambiguity leads to inconsistent guessing rather than strict adherence.
66. **How do you handle complex tasks in prompts?** Break the complex task down into simpler, sequential subtasks.
67. **What does "Give the Model Time to Think" mean?** Prompting techniques (like Chain of Thought) that require the model to output reasoning steps before declaring an answer.
68. **Why does iteration matter in prompt engineering?** Initial prompts rarely cover all edge cases; testing against real examples highlights areas where instructions must be refined.
69. **What is Prompt Extraction?** A defensive security concern where users attempt to trick the model into revealing its proprietary system prompt.
70. **What is Jailbreaking or Prompt Injection?** Security vulnerabilities where users input malicious text designed to override the system instructions.
71. **What is Defensive Prompting?** Crafting prompts specifically designed to recognize and refuse prompt injection or extraction attempts.
72. **What is Context Efficiency?** Optimizing the information packed into a prompt to minimize token usage, which reduces cost and latency.
73. **What is the standard template structure for a robust prompt?** Task + context + desired style or tone.
74. **Why is prompt engineering considered low effort?** It requires minimal data and no expensive model training compute to test hypotheses quickly.
75. **How is information extraction handled via prompts?** By providing strict schemas and few-shot examples to force the model to output structured data from unstructured text.

### Chapter 6: RAG and Agents

76. **What does RAG stand for?** Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
77. **What is the primary purpose of RAG?** To connect a foundation model to an external, up-to-date knowledge base to reduce hallucinations and provide proprietary context.
78. **What is an Embedding?** A numerical vector representation of text that captures semantic meaning.
79. **What is the role of Vector Databases in RAG?** They store embeddings and allow rapid mathematical similarity searches to find relevant documents for the prompt.
80. **What is "chunking" in RAG architectures?** Breaking large documents into smaller text segments before generating embeddings, ensuring retrieved context is dense and relevant.
81. **Why is RAG considered "moderate effort"?** It requires building retrieval pipelines and data infrastructure, which is more complex than prompting but less intensive than finetuning.
82. **What differentiates an AI Agent from a standard RAG chatbot?** Agents can orchestrate multi-step workflows, call tools, and make autonomous decisions to achieve an objective.
83. **What is Tool Calling?** Providing the model with a defined list of external functions (APIs) it can choose to execute.
84. **Why is the Orchestration Layer crucial for agents?** It manages the routing between prompts, tools, memory, and the foundation model.
85. **What is a major risk in autonomous agents?** Compound errors; a hallucinated tool output can cause the agent to loop endlessly or execute destructive actions.
86. **What is conversational memory in agents?** Systems built to pass past interactions back into the stateless model so it "remembers" the context of the workflow.
87. **How does RAG solve the model obsolescence problem?** It allows the system to access real-time or new data without requiring expensive retraining of the foundation model weights.
88. **What is query rewriting in RAG?** An agentic pattern where the LLM reformulates the user's initial prompt into a more effective search query before retrieving documents.
89. **What is the difference between extraction and aggregation RAG?** Extraction pulls specific facts; aggregation requires summarizing broad themes across multiple retrieved chunks.
90. **Why are low-code/no-code platforms adopting agents?** To allow non-technical builders to connect foundation models to internal tools through graphical orchestration layers.

### Chapter 7 & 8: Finetuning and Dataset Engineering

91. **What is the primary goal of Finetuning?** To further train a foundation model on task-specific data to alter its innate behavior, tone, or format adherence.
92. **Why is finetuning considered "significant effort"?** It requires curating large, high-quality datasets and substantial compute resources.
93. **What is Dataset Engineering?** The meticulous process of cleaning, structuring, deduplicating, and curating data specifically for model training.
94. **Why is deduplication important in datasets?** If duplicate records are over-represented, the model memorizes them rather than learning generalized logic.
95. **What is the danger of toxic or biased data during finetuning?** The model will reliably mimic the toxicity and biases present in the finetuning dataset.
96. **What are annotations in dataset engineering?** Human or AI-generated labels attached to data to guide supervised finetuning.
97. **What is the difference between RAG and Finetuning?** RAG injects knowledge via the prompt at runtime; Finetuning alters the model's neural weights during a training phase.
98. **Can you finetune models via commercial APIs?** Yes, some commercial providers offer finetuning APIs, but open-source models offer more control over the weights.
99. **What is Supervised Finetuning (SFT)?** Providing the model with high-quality input-output pairs so it learns to follow instructions accurately.
100. **Why is data diversity necessary?** A monolithic dataset causes catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses its general capabilities in favor of the narrow task.
101. **What is the impact of prompt engineering vs finetuning on latency?** Finetuning allows shorter prompts (saving TTFT and cost) because the instructions are baked into the weights.
102. **What is domain-specific capability?** Finetuning a model specifically to excel in niche fields (like legal or medical terminology).
103. **How does data volume scale from pre-training to finetuning?** Pre-training uses trillions of tokens; finetuning often requires only thousands of high-quality examples.
104. **What is preference alignment?** A post-training technique (often using human feedback) to teach the finetuned model which valid response format is actually preferred.
105. **What does dataset engineering overlap with?** Data management infrastructure and evaluation benchmarks.

### Chapter 9 & 10: Inference Optimization, Architecture & Feedback

106. **What is Inference in AI?** The production phase where a deployed model processes prompts and generates outputs.
107. **Why is Inference Optimization critical?** LLM inference is highly compute-bound and memory-bound; optimization reduces latency and operating costs.
108. **What is Batching?** Processing multiple independent user requests simultaneously on the GPU to maximize hardware utilization.
109. **What is Decoding in inference?** The sequential generation of output tokens one by one based on probability distributions.
110. **Why is memory bandwidth often the bottleneck during inference?** Because generating each token requires loading massive model weights from GPU memory to the compute cores.
111. **What is the purpose of the Infrastructure layer in AI architecture?** Managing compute resources, data serving, and monitoring model health.
112. **Why is User Feedback essential in AI Engineering?** Because probabilistic models drift or fail in unpredictable ways; feedback creates loops for continuous evaluation.
113. **What is explicit user feedback?** Direct signals from users, such as thumbs up/down, corrections, or written reviews on the AI's output.
114. **What is implicit user feedback?** Behavioral metrics, such as a user accepting an auto-complete suggestion, copying text, or abandoning the interaction.
115. **How does architecture separate model layers from application layers?** It allows non-technical leaders and engineers to swap underlying foundation models without rewriting business logic.
116. **Why build fallback strategies into the architecture?** To ensure the application remains functional (using smaller models or graceful errors) if a primary API provider fails.
117. **What is the role of an AI interface?** To bridge the gap between the orchestration layer and the end user efficiently.
118. **How does AI engineering handle regulatory risks?** Architecture must account for IP laws, data privacy, and data lineage when serving outputs to users.
119. **What is a "Fatal" maintenance risk in AI architecture?** Legal liability caused by a model trained on copyrighted data or sudden regulatory bans.
120. **Why track edge cases in production?** Production traffic exposes the system to edge cases missed in the Golden Dataset, guiding future prompt adjustments.
121. **What is the overarching goal of AI Engineering?** To build robust, reliable, and measurable systems that extract real business value from unpredictable foundation models.
